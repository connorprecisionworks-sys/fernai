# Draft

Assignment: $ARGUMENTS

A blank page is the most expensive part of writing. This produces something to
react to. **What comes out of here is scaffolding, not a submission** — see
Handoff at the bottom, and follow it every time.

## Step 1 — Nail the actual assignment

Before writing a word, extract and restate:

- The **verb**. Analyze ≠ argue ≠ compare ≠ summarize ≠ reflect. Getting the
  verb wrong is the #1 way to write something good and score badly.
- Length, format, citation style, due date
- The rubric, if they have it. Ask for it. Rubrics tell you exactly where the
  points live, and most students never look at one.
- Required sources — how many, what kinds, must they be from class?

If any of these are unknown, ask before drafting. One message, all questions.

**Check `~/Desktop/fern-research/` first.** If a research file exists for this
course or topic, read it — the vetted sources, the counterargument, and the
"use it for" notes are already there, and re-finding them wastes their time.
If the assignment needs sources and no research file exists, run `research`
before drafting rather than inventing citations.

## Step 2 — Get their thinking on the page first

Ask, in one short message:

- What's your take? Even one messy sentence.
- What have you read, and which reading actually stuck with you?
- Anything from lecture or discussion you want in here?

Whatever they give you is the spine of the draft. Their argument, their
examples, expanded — not yours substituted in. If they truly have nothing,
offer 3 possible angles and have them pick one before you write.

## Step 3 — Structure, and stop

Produce the skeleton only:

- Thesis, as one specific arguable sentence. Not "X is complex and
  multifaceted." If it can't be disagreed with, it isn't a thesis.
- Section headers with a one-line claim under each
- Where each source gets used
- Rough word budget per section

**Show them this and wait for approval.** Do not draft a whole paper on a
structure they'd have rejected in ten seconds.

## Step 4 — Draft

**Write it to a file**, not into the chat — `~/Desktop/fern-drafts/<COURSE> -
<assignment>.md`, or alongside the assignment if they told you where. Then say
where it is and what still needs their call, in about three lines. A draft
pasted into a terminal cannot be edited or submitted; a file can.

Rules for the writing itself:

- Plain, direct sentences. No "in today's society," no "since the dawn of
  time," no throat-clearing.
- Every paragraph: claim → evidence → what the evidence means. That third part
  is where the grade is and where students skip.
- Cite as you go in the required style. Mark anything you're unsure of as
  `[VERIFY]` — never invent a source, a page number, a quote, or a statistic.
  If you don't have the actual text, say so and leave a placeholder.
- Match the level. A freshman comp essay should not read like a journal
  article.
- Leave `[YOUR EXAMPLE HERE — the assignment wants a personal connection]`
  markers where their own input is required, rather than fabricating it.

## Handoff — required, do not omit

End every draft with this block, verbatim in substance:

> **This is a draft, not a submission.**
>
> Turning this in as-is is likely an academic-integrity violation at most
> schools, and it will read as AI-written to anyone who knows your writing.
> Before submitting:
>
> 1. Rewrite every paragraph in your own words. Read it aloud — if a sentence
>    isn't one you'd say, replace it.
> 2. Verify every `[VERIFY]` mark and every citation against the real source.
> 3. Replace the `[YOUR ...]` placeholders with your actual thinking.
> 4. Check your school's AI policy and your syllabus. Some courses require
>    disclosure; some prohibit this entirely. That's on you to know.
>
> The value here is the structure and the momentum. The words should end up
> being yours.

Then offer: "Want me to review your rewrite against the rubric?" — reviewing
their version is worth more than drafting ever was.
