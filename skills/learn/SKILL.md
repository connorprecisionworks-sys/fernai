# Learn

Target: $ARGUMENTS

Most learning plans fail the same way: too much passive input, no artifact, no
deadline. This produces the opposite.

## Step 1 — Force specificity

"Learn Python" is not a goal, it's a mood. Convert it into:

- **A thing you will have built or be able to do**, checkable by someone else
- **A deadline**
- **Hours per week**, realistically — ask what else is on their plate

Ask for these. If they can't name the artifact, propose three and have them
pick. Everything downstream depends on this.

## Step 2 — Find the minimum viable core

Every field has a small core that unlocks most of the practical work, and a
long tail almost no beginner needs. Name both explicitly:

- **Core (learn now):** the 20% that appears in nearly every real task
- **Tail (learn on demand, when you hit it):** the rest — say plainly that
  looking these up later is correct, not a gap

Be aggressive about what goes in the tail. Most curricula are padded because
they're written for completeness, not for someone with six weeks.

## Step 3 — Project-first sequence

Structure it as a chain of 3–5 builds, each shippable, each forcing new
concepts. Not chapters. Not a video series.

```
Build 1  — smallest working thing, day 1–3, must run/work by end of day 3
Build 2  — adds <concept>, week 1
Build 3  — adds <concept>, week 2–3
Build 4  — the actual goal artifact
```

Rule: something works at the end of every session. A broken half-thing kills
momentum harder than a small ugly finished thing.

## Step 4 — Resources, ruthlessly filtered

Pick **one** primary resource. Not a list of twelve. Resource-shopping is
procrastination that feels like progress.

- 1 primary (a book, a course, or official docs — pick the one matching their
  learning style, ask if unclear)
- 1 reference to look things up in
- 1 place to get unstuck (a specific forum, discord, subreddit, or "ask me")

Name actual specific resources where you know good ones. If you're not sure a
resource currently exists or is still good, say so rather than guessing at a
URL.

## Step 5 — The schedule

Map builds onto their real calendar with dates. Include:

- Session length and how many per week
- A **weekly checkpoint**: one question they must be able to answer or one
  thing that must run. Written down in advance.
- One deliberate rest day. Plans without slack break on contact with week 1.

## Step 6 — Anti-patterns for this specific skill

Name the 3–5 traps for *this* domain, concretely. Examples of the genre:
tutorial hell, learning the framework before the language, memorizing syntax
instead of writing code, practicing the easy parts because they feel good,
switching resources at the first difficulty.

## Rules baked into every plan

- Active > passive. Reading about it is not learning it. Cap passive input at
  ~30% of total time.
- Retrieve, don't review. Close the book and reproduce it from memory.
- Struggle before help. 15 minutes stuck, then look it up. Getting the answer
  too fast means it doesn't stick.
- Spaced, not massed. Four 45-minute sessions beat one 3-hour session, always.
- Ship publicly. A deadline someone else can see is worth more than
  motivation.

## Output

A one-page plan: goal artifact, core vs. tail, build sequence with dates, the
one primary resource, weekly checkpoints, the traps.

Then: **what to do in the next 30 minutes.** Specific. Today.
