# Email

Situation: $ARGUMENTS

Professors get 100+ emails a day and read them on a phone between classes.
Every rule below exists because of that one fact.

## Get these before writing

- Who: name, and their actual title (Professor / Dr. / Dean — never "Mr./Mrs."
  for faculty, never first name unless they've signed off that way)
- Course code and section
- What you want, as a specific action they can take
- Relevant dates and any prior contact
- Is this late/awkward? If yes, name it in the email rather than hoping they
  won't notice.

Ask only for what's missing. Then write.

## The shape

**Subject line:** course code + the ask. `BIOL 201 — Extension request for Lab 4`.
They triage on this alone.

**First sentence:** who you are and the ask. Not a windup.
> "I'm Connor Reyes in your Tuesday 2pm BIOL 201 section. I'm writing to ask
> for a two-day extension on Lab 4."

**Middle, 2–4 sentences max:** the reason, stated once, plainly, no
performance. Then what you've already done about it — this is the part that
separates a granted request from a denied one. Show effort before asking for
grace.

**The ask, made easy to grant:** propose a specific solution.
> "Could I submit by Friday at 5pm? I'd still make Monday's discussion."

A yes/no question gets answered. An open-ended plea gets deferred.

**Close:** thanks, full name, student ID if it's admin.

Total: under 150 words. If it's longer, cut.

## Tone

- Respectful, not groveling. You're a student making a reasonable request, not
  a supplicant. Excessive apology reads as weakness and wastes their time.
- No sob story unless it's true and load-bearing, and then one sentence.
  Medical/family details: "a family emergency" is enough; you don't owe them
  specifics and offering too many reads as overselling.
- Never blame the professor, the syllabus, or another student.
- Never lie. It's the one mistake with no recovery.

## Situational notes

**Extensions** — ask *before* the deadline, always. Before is a request; after
is a confession. If it's already late, lead with acknowledgment: "I missed the
deadline on X and I'm not asking you to ignore that."

**Grade disputes** — never argue the grade. Ask about the reasoning. "Could you
help me understand what a stronger answer to Q3 would have looked like?" This
gets points back more often than demanding them, and it doesn't burn the
relationship. Wait 24 hours before sending. Always.

**Rec letters** — ask 4+ weeks out. Include: what it's for, the deadline, how
to submit, your resume, the specific class/project where they knew you, and an
explicit out ("if you don't feel you could write a strong letter, I completely
understand"). That last line is not optional — it's what makes the ask
professional.

**Research / lab positions** — one specific paper of theirs you actually read,
one sentence on what interested you, what you can do, how many hours a week.
Attach a resume. Generic mass emails get zero replies.

**Awkward replies** (you were wrong, missed something, got called out) —
acknowledge in the first line without excuses, state the fix, propose the next
step. Short. Do not over-explain; length reads as guilt.

## Output

Give the subject line and body, ready to send. Then one line: what you'd change
if their professor is known to be strict vs. relaxed. Offer to adjust tone —
don't produce three variants unasked.
